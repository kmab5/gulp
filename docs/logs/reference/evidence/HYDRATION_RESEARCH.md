# Hydration research synthesis · 2026-10-19

## Executive finding
A single universal number and exact optimum drinking interval are **not evidence-based**. The scientifically responsible product is an adult daily-routine assistant with nonprescriptive references, flexible cues, easy tracking, and transparent uncertainty.

## Reference intakes
**EFSA (2010):** adult females 2.0 L/day and males 2.5 L/day, referring to **total water** from drinking water, all beverages, and food, under stated reference circumstances. Source: https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459

**US National Academies (2005):** adult females 2.7 L/day and males 3.7 L/day **total water**. These are population adequate-intake reference figures, not personalized mandatory goals; don't blindly map from one authority to another. Source: https://doi.org/10.17226/10925

Food often contributes around 20–30% of water under ordinary diets. A simple illustration using 75% from beverages implies 1,500 mL/day and 1,875 mL/day for the EFSA groups, respectively. These numbers are the **app team's arithmetic** and NOT EFSA's distinct beverage prescriptions; food composition and climate matter. There is no automatic requirement to set a numerical goal.

## Needs are variable
Yamada et al. measured water turnover using isotope data from **5,604 people** across ages and climates. Age, composition, activity, temperature, and other variables matter. Water turnover is not identical to a prescribed bottle target. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abm8668

Heat, heavy exercise, vomiting/diarrhea, pregnancy/breastfeeding and clinician-supervised conditions can alter needs. Do not implement automatic fixed '+X mL on a hot day' without validation.

## Other beverages
Tea, coffee, and milk contribute fluid. Killer et al.'s modest coffee study found no meaningful hydration difference between moderate coffee and water among habitual coffee drinkers under measured conditions. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0084154

Maughan et al.'s short-term beverage hydration index investigation describes differences in urine output/retention for certain drinks—not evidence that every coffee should be arbitrarily discounted. https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.115.114769

Track ordinary beverage **volume** without fake 'hydration multipliers'. Water is the app's simple default, and counted fluid does not imply nutritional equivalence. Food moisture is not estimated in v1; report 'logged beverages', not 'complete total water intake'.

## Reminder interventions and behavioral change
Reeves et al. 2023 randomized pilot: 22 adult women assigned reminder-tracker app or motivational bottle for five weeks; reported water intake increased roughly 29%, while objective hydration indicators were not reliably improved. This is **small preliminary evidence** for behavioral reminders, not proof an interval is optimal. https://doi.org/10.53520/jen2023.103142

Lally et al. 2010 studied real-world habit formation; contextual repetition promotes automaticity, timing varies, and one missed instance need not destroy habit acquisition. This supports contextual reminders and no punitive streaks—not any exact hydration dose. https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674

## Clinical outcomes should not become consumer marketing
Hooton et al., 2018: in **140 women** with recurrent uncomplicated cystitis and low usual fluid intake, an additional 1.5 L/day water intervention decreased recurrences compared with controls. Stronger evidence for this **specific clinical population**; it cannot justify giving all users identical water instructions. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2018.4204

Hakam et al., 2024, reviewed 18 randomized trials of changed water intake. Evidence across diagnoses was limited and heterogeneous; some studied outcomes were promising but do not warrant universal consumer health promises. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.47621

Desai et al. PUSH randomized hydration-adherence intervention trial, Lancet 2026 (approximately 1,658 participants): the intervention did not significantly improve symptomatic kidney stone recurrence against guideline-based care over the studied period. Interpretation requires attention to achieved urine-volume targets, not an assertion that water never matters. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(25)02637-6

## Overdrinking and low sodium
Exercise-associated hyponatremia can be dangerous. During prolonged activity users must not be told to replace arbitrary numbers of missed glasses; thirst-responsive guidance is more appropriate than forced drinking. Hew-Butler et al., 2015 consensus: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-095004

## Urine color / thirst
Can be rough contextual indicators, not sufficient standalone diagnostic tests; confounded by drugs/vitamins and less reliable in some populations. Optional check-in only, no clinical diagnosis. Urine color review: https://doi.org/10.1080/07315724.2020.1750073 ; contextual guidance https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/food-guidelines-and-food-labels/water-drinks-nutrition/

## Product implications / boundaries
1. Offer reminder-only mode and optional drink-recording reference.
2. Permit user-selected interval; preset 90 minutes is **UX only**, not science-determined.
3. Remind only during awake hours; suppress repeat prompts and respect dismissal.
4. Never demand missed-volume catch-up or incentivize higher intake to unlock visuals.
5. Track logged beverages, never diagnose hydration percentage or assert prevention of disease.
6. Users with fluid restrictions/specialized plans can record clinician-provided goals but app cannot autonomously prescribe.
7. Don't use infant/child algorithms in v1; adults 18+ only.
8. All health claims/features pass a citation, scope, limitations, safety and content review before shipping.

## Evidence grades
**A (formal authority / high-quality synthesis):** EFSA, NASEM, meta-reviews; authoritative but not individual prescription.
**B (relevant RCT or controlled trial):** Hooton, PUSH, selected beverage studies; population/outcome specific.
**C (preliminary behavior evidence):** small reminder pilot, behavioral design extrapolations.
**P (product convention):** intervals, colors, motion, rewards, adaptive suppressions; test with users, never cite as established medical fact.
